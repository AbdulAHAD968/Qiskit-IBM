Which relatives do we test?

A planning tool for beta-thalassaemia cascade screening in Pakistan. It picks the relatives whose tests are expected to reveal the most at-risk couples, using a QAOA circuit in Qiskit that cannot break the kit limit, and it checks every answer against the exact optimum.

1The problem in Pakistan

Thalassaemia runs in families. Test kits and field visits do not stretch that far.

Thalassaemia major appears when both parents are carriers. Screening teams start from a family that already has an affected child and test the relatives. This is cascade screening. Kits, laboratory capacity and home visits are limited, so on every visit someone has to choose who gets tested.

Cascade screening finds couples

In the study that introduced it in Pakistan, 17 of 214 married couples in families with an affected child were both carriers, which is 8 percent. Among 350 randomly chosen pregnant women there was no at-risk couple at all.1

Field capacity is limited

The Punjab Thalassaemia Prevention Project covers all 36 districts of Punjab from nine regional centres with 48 field officers, and reports that uptake of cascade testing among relatives is low.2

The law is moving

In 2026 the National Assembly and the Senate passed a compulsory thalassaemia screening bill for the Islamabad Capital Territory. It asks for premarital screening reports and for screening and counselling of patients' blood relatives.4

Who it serves. Field officers planning one home visit, and programme managers spreading kits across families. Every family in this project is synthetic and seeded. No patient data is used anywhere.

2Why it is an optimisation problem

The value sits on couples, not on individuals.

An at-risk couple can only be confirmed when both partners are tested. One test can rule a couple out, but it can never confirm one. So the best choice depends on pairs of people, and the obvious rule, test whoever is most likely to be a carrier, spends kits on people whose partners go untested.

One kit used. No at-risk couple can be confirmed.
Two kits used. At-risk couple found with probability w.

Pair risk is not a product of single risks

The father of an affected child is a carrier, so almost surely exactly one of his own parents is. Each grandparent alone is a carrier with probability 51%, yet both together only with probability 2.6%.

3Formulation

Choose exactly k relatives to maximise the expected number of at-risk couples found.

maxx ∈ {0,1}n   ∑(i,j) ∈ C cij wij xi xj  +  λ ∑i ri pi xi subject to   ∑i xi = k

expected at-risk couples found, exact and not an approximation    a small reward for single carriers found

Variables, objective, constraint

Where the weights come from

A pedigree engine applies Mendel's rules to the family tree, given the affected child and a 5 percent population carrier rate, and computes every probability exactly, loops from cousin marriages included. It is checked against textbook values before any optimisation runs.

    5Quantum method

    A circuit that can only output plans with exactly k kits.

    The objective is a sum over pairs of yes-or-no decisions, which is the native form of the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm.7 The kit limit is enforced by the structure of the circuit, as in the quantum alternating operator ansatz, so it needs no penalty term.8, 5

    Step 1

    One qubit per relative

    Measuring a qubit as 1 means "test this person". A measured bitstring is a complete screening plan. Ten relatives need ten qubits.

    Step 2

    Start in a Dicke state

    The start state is an equal superposition of every plan that uses exactly k kits and nothing else. Qiskit has no built-in Dicke state, so the circuit is built from gates with the deterministic scheme of Bärtschi and Eidenbenz: order k·n gates and no helper qubits.6

    Step 3

    Cost layer on the couple graph

    One ZZ rotation per couple link and one Z rotation per relative. Each plan picks up a phase proportional to its objective value. A test confirms that the circuit energy equals the objective for every one of the 2n inputs.

    Step 4

    XY ring mixer

    Each XX+YY rotation swaps a tested and an untested neighbour. The number of ones never changes, so the circuit moves amplitude between valid plans only.9 Cost and mixer are repeated p times.

    Step 5

    Tune, then measure

    The Estimator primitive returns the expected objective, COBYLA tunes the 2p angles, and the Sampler primitive reads out plans. Deeper circuits start from the tuned angles of the previous depth.10 The best measured plan goes to the field officer.

    Baseline: penalty QAOA

    Standard QAOA starts from all 2n bitstrings and subtracts a penalty A·(∑xi − k)2 for using the wrong number of kits. That needs a ZZ rotation on every pair of qubits, and the optimiser spends its effort on the penalty. At depth 3 with 16 qubits only of its shots are valid plans.

    How hard is the problem?

    With equal weights it is the densest-k-subgraph problem, which is NP-hard, and the best known efficient algorithm only guarantees a factor of about n1/4.12 That is a worst-case statement about general graphs. Family graphs are sparse, and section 8 says plainly what that means for the instances here.

    Our one-sentence answer to "why quantum"We do not claim a speedup. We show a problem QAOA represents directly, a circuit that cannot break the kit limit, and an honest comparison against exact answers.
    6Architecture

    Classical code prepares and checks. The quantum circuit proposes the plans.

    Parts 1 to 4 and 6 to 7 are classical Python. Part 5 is the quantum component: the circuit is built, tuned and sampled through Qiskit primitives on the Aer simulator. The plan that reaches the field officer is a measured bitstring from that circuit.

    8Limitations and what did not work

    What the numbers do not show.

    No quantum speedup

    Brute force gives the exact answer for the 10- and 16-qubit instances in milliseconds, and simulated annealing found the best plan in of the 16-qubit families. At these sizes a classical solver wins. Tuning the angles for one 16-qubit family alone took about circuit evaluations, to choose among 4,368 plans. This is a method study on a new problem.

    Family graphs are sparse

    The couple graph splits into small pieces: the largest connected component has people on average in the 16-relative families (at most ). An exact dynamic programme over those pieces solves the problem, so worst-case hardness results do not apply to these instances.

    Expected quality is modest

    At depth 3 constrained QAOA reaches on 16 qubits. A classical greedy heuristic reaches . QAOA beats the field rule and random picks, not the stronger classical heuristics.

    Penalty QAOA barely beats random

    Its valid shots score against for a random valid plan on 16 qubits, and of its shots break the kit limit at depth 3.

    A cheaper start state failed

    Starting from the field officer's plan instead of the Dicke state needs only k X gates, but at depth 3 its chance of the best plan was against for the Dicke start, on small instances.

    Noise costs most of the shots

    The Dicke start state alone takes CZ gates on 10 qubits. At 1 percent two-qubit error only of constrained shots still use exactly k kits.

    Simulator only

    No real quantum processor was used. The noise model is generic and assumes all-to-all connectivity; on a line of qubits the depth-1 circuit grows from to CZ gates.

    Sampling flatters small instances

    With thousands of shots and only 210 or 4,368 valid plans, even uniform sampling usually contains the best plan. That is why the charts report the chance per shot and the expected quality, not the best of all shots.

    Synthetic families, simple genetics

    Families are generated, not observed. The average carrier share among testable relatives is against 31 percent in the 2002 study. The model covers one visit, one gene, a perfect test, and no step-by-step testing. The kit budget as the binding limit and the field officer's rule as the baseline are our assumptions, not programme protocol.

    Ethics

    A tool that ranks relatives by genetic risk can stigmatise people. This one uses synthetic data only, treats consent as an input (anyone who declines is fixed to "not tested"), publishes no individual scores, and is meant to support a counsellor's plan for one visit, not to label people or to decide who may marry.

    What we would do next

    Ask a thalassaemia centre whether kits, laboratory slots or officer time is the real limit; model step-by-step testing where one result updates the next choice; calibrate families against programme data; and run the smallest instance on a real IBM processor with angles trained on the simulator.
